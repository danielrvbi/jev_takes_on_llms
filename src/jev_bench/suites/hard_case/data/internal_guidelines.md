# EuroBank Assurance Europe — motor claim handling guide (fictional extract)

**Document:** Retail Motor Claims Practice Note MN-4.8  
**Version date:** 1 July 2026  
**Applies to:** Veyra retail motor claims handlers and supervisors  
**Purpose:** Internal workflow guidance. This document is not law, legal advice, a coverage decision, or a substitute for the operative wording, schedule, regulatory requirements, or manager authority. All businesses and records in this packet are fictional.

## 1. Separate evidence from conclusions

For each material fact, record the source and status. Use plain labels such as:

- **Known from a record:** supported by a document or system field whose provenance is identified;
- **Reported:** stated by a customer, driver, witness, or repairer and attributed to that source;
- **Observed:** directly seen or measured by the handler or named expert;
- **Inferred:** a reasoned interpretation that remains open to alternatives;
- **Unresolved:** the file lacks enough information to decide.

Do not turn a portal tick box into a verified fact. Preserve an original statement when a customer later corrects it. Corrections may increase reliability by clarifying a vague first account, or may leave a material question; judge the specific point. Do not silently replace earlier details, merge statements from different people, or describe a disputed fact as “confirmed” merely because it appears in a police or repair record.

An incident record may establish that an officer attended and recorded what people said. It does not automatically establish right of way, speed, signal use, point of impact, or legal responsibility. A repair estimate establishes that a shop proposed work at a price; it does not establish accident causation, necessity, completion, or payment.

## 2. When to ask for customer clarification

Ask a focused question when the answer can materially affect cover, liability, damage scope, or the next handling step. Prefer one issue at a time and explain why the information is needed. Examples include:

- who was driving, where the vehicle was coming from, and whether the account is based on direct observation;
- whether a journey was personal, commuting, or undertaken as a paid task, where the wording makes purpose relevant;
- whether an item was being carried for the insured or delivered for someone else;
- the position and movement of vehicles, indicator timing, traffic controls, or when photographs were taken;
- whether old damage was repaired, worsened in the new event, or is simply included in a combined estimate;
- whether an invoice is an estimate, provisional account, paid bill, or correction;
- what a duplicate or unexplained line represents, and whether a part was removed or a procedure completed;
- an unclear date, mileage reading, contact detail, or event reference where the discrepancy matters.

A customer may answer “I don't remember.” That is a usable answer. Do not demand certainty where memory or records cannot supply it. Do not request broad private records, unrelated account transactions, location history, or online-sales history unless a specific, proportionate question remains material and a supervisor agrees. A parcel, uniform, work vehicle nearby, or prior claim may justify a question when relevant, but does not by itself prove excluded use or dishonesty.

When information is missing, ask whether it exists, who may hold it, and whether the person can reasonably obtain it. Keep “not supplied,” “not available,” and “does not exist” distinct. Consider an alternative such as a repairer record, original photo metadata, police reference, or short passenger account before repeating the same request.

## 3. When to consult policy wording

Consult the operative schedule, wording, and endorsement record before applying a definition, exclusion, excess, driver restriction, or use condition. A product summary, generic portal banner, previous claim outcome, or internal shorthand cannot replace the contract record. Check that the correct version was in force on the event date and that any amendment is actually attached to the policy.

Policy grounding is required before communicating any of the following as a decision or likely outcome:

- whether an accident is within the covered period or territory;
- whether a driver is eligible;
- whether a journey falls within declared use or a business-use exclusion;
- whether pre-existing damage, wear, betterment, or a prior payment affects the current scope;
- what excess applies and whether it is deducted once or more than once;
- whether notice, cooperation, repair, or evidence conditions affect handling;
- whether emergency work is within a monetary allowance;
- whether the claim should be paid, limited, declined, or reserved.

Quote the relevant section in the file and explain the connection between the wording and established facts. If a material fact is unresolved, state what remains unresolved and what evidence could narrow it. Do not present a provisional screen response or this guide as a binding policy interpretation.

## 4. Human review and authority

Refer to a supervisor or technical reviewer before a final adverse coverage decision, contested liability position, or communication that could prejudice a recovery right. Human review is also expected when:

- two or more materially different accident accounts remain and evidence does not distinguish them;
- the proposed repair total exceeds the handler's delegated limit (retail desk authority is €5,000 net for current-impact repair in this edition of the guide);
- a work order combines current and prior damage and the allocation affects settlement;
- the proposed scope involves structural measurement, calibration, safety-critical steering work, or an unusual repair method;
- an exclusion may apply but a material purpose, driver, timing, or causation fact is disputed;
- information might support a special-investigation referral, but the evidentiary threshold or proportionality is uncertain;
- the record contains a material complaint, vulnerability, injury, or regulatory issue requiring a specialist process.

A referral does not decide the outcome. Include a concise issue statement, evidence for and against each reasonable account, wording sections, customer answer, missing information, and the decision needed. Do not ask a reviewer to “confirm fraud” from a bundle of inconsistencies. Ask for an evidence-based review of whether more enquiries are proportionate and what neutral customer question, if any, remains.

## 5. Fraud and integrity indicators

Potential indicators are prompts for careful verification, not findings. Examples can include material changes to a central account, duplicate payment requests, documents with unexplained alterations, implausible timing, evidence that a prior loss is being presented as new, or a knowingly false statement on a material point. Indicators must be considered in context, including whether a person corrected a mistake voluntarily, whether the document is a draft or system-generated, and whether a reasonable explanation is available.

The following are not enough on their own to label a claim suspicious: one prior claim; a cash settlement; a customer lacking an old invoice; a small mileage discrepancy; a misspelled name; a repair estimate with a duplicated line that the repairer acknowledges; a vehicle being near a commercial operator; a parcel in a car; a difference between a first recollection and a later clarification; or disputed liability. Multiple low-specificity facts do not become proof merely by being listed together.

If a specific potential indicator remains after ordinary clarification, refer through the Claims Integrity Liaison (CIL) queue using neutral language. Do not tell the customer that they are under investigation, coach them to alter an account, suggest a preferred version, or threaten a policy consequence. Continue ordinary claim handling where appropriate. Preserve original documents and corrections. Any decision to limit, decline, or report externally must be authorized and grounded in the policy and applicable process; this guide does not establish legal thresholds.

## 6. Evidence and documentation

For a two-vehicle collision, seek proportionate evidence: both drivers' accounts, vehicle positions, scene and damage photos, witness contact details if available, police reference or incident record, relevant video if retained, and repairer observations. Ask when images were taken and whether vehicles had moved. Record the source, date received, image limitations, and any uncertainty. Avoid treating damage geometry as a complete reconstruction unless a qualified reviewer has made one.

For vehicle repair, request an itemized estimate that identifies parts, operations, hours, rates, paint/materials, diagnostics/calibration, towing, storage, tax, and any prior-damage work. Ask the repairer to reconcile duplicate or corrected lines and distinguish estimate from completed or paid work. Preserve pre-strip photographs and parts where reasonable. A diagnostic recommendation does not prove that calibration is needed; ask for a model-specific procedure, scan report, or technical explanation. Compare the estimate against vehicle age, condition, safety, repairability, and documented impact.

When a prior claim affects the same panel, check its date, stated cause, photographs, settlement basis, and any repair verification. Establish whether new damage is separately visible or whether a blend is technically necessary. Do not infer that a customer received a prior payment for a completed repair unless the record says so. A cash settlement often has no repair invoice by design.

For vehicle location and mileage, keep estimates, intake readings, inspection readings, and customer photographs separate. Check original image metadata or contemporaneous service records where proportionate. A single inconsistent odometer entry may be a typo; do not frame it as tampering without stronger evidence.

## 7. Liability and communication

Use provisional terms such as “accounts differ,” “not yet established,” or “the available material does not resolve who moved first.” Do not make a legal conclusion from an internal claim note. If a customer asks whether the other driver was at fault, explain what the current evidence shows and what remains to be obtained. Preserve recovery rights and avoid admissions on behalf of EBAE.

Record the customer's direct observations separately from what a passenger, witness, police officer, or repairer said. A passenger who looked away at impact can still describe what happened before or after, but the limit of observation should be explicit. A post-event comment by an unidentified witness is weaker than a named account of the impact sequence; it is not necessarily irrelevant.

Keep correspondence short, neutral, and clear. Ask the customer not to guess and say that “not remembered” is acceptable. Explain that an information request is not itself a coverage finding. If an earlier request was unclear or too broad, narrow it and record the correction.

## 8. Safety, privacy, and customer care

If a vehicle may be unsafe to drive, advise the customer not to drive it pending inspection and discuss reasonable transport or towing channels. Do not encourage a road test by an unqualified person. Ask the repairer to preserve replaced components and diagnostics where feasible.

Collect only information reasonably needed for the claim. A customer may redact unrelated transactions when supplying a payment record. Store sensitive material only in the approved claim record. Do not send a police report or another driver's personal details to a party who is not entitled to receive it.

Escalate an immediate injury, safety threat, suspected evidence destruction, serious vulnerability, or privacy incident through the applicable team. The present case's file summary records no injury and no such immediate emergency, but handlers should check updated information before relying on that summary.

## 9. Suggested record format

For each open issue, use: **Question / why material / current evidence / evidence against / clarification tried / result / next action / authority needed.** A concise example for a mixed repair estimate is:

> Question: does the right-rear door work arise from the September impact or a necessary blend? Why material: June file records the same area and a prior cash settlement. Current evidence: June photos and September photos show a mark in the same broad area; estimate separates rear work as customer-requested. Evidence against a new rear impact: FNOL identifies only left-front contact; police observed right-rear mark but did not date it. Next action: obtain repairer's paint/blend map and clean revised estimate; retain the rear item outside the September scope pending review.

This format organizes evidence; it does not dictate the decision.
